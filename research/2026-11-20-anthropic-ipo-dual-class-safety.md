## 1. Question
Does Anthropic’s dual-class IPO structure (Founder LLC Class F share carrying a fixed 50.1% of general voting power, plus Long-Term Benefit Trust electing board majority) give the co-founders durable capacity to enforce the “we will not bend to either business or political pressure” safety pacing pledge Dario Amodei restated on the listing day, or does the public float + $900bn+ compute commitments create a structural contradiction that markets will eventually force?

## 7. Contradictions
- Amodei’s “will not bend” pledge vs. the existence of a public float whose price will move daily on growth/earnings news. The dual-class structure is explicitly designed to reduce that pressure, yet the company still has $900bn+ of committed spend that must be financed; a sustained stock decline could still constrain capital raises even with voting control.
- Revenue growth is extraordinary (1,000%+ in places), yet the company continues to report large GAAP losses driven by non-cash charges and heavy compute spend. Public markets may reward growth for a while, but the same markets have punished other AI names on capex concerns in recent weeks.
- “Race to the top” requires being at the frontier; being at the frontier requires continuous large training runs. The structure protects the decision to slow down, but does not remove the competitive and financial incentives to keep going.
- Founders’ economic ownership is modest (~low single digits each); their control is legal/structural rather than aligned purely through personal equity. This is intentional but creates a classic dual-class critique that public shareholders bear residual risk without residual control.

## 8. Mechanism
The IPO is the first major pure-play frontier lab to list. Anthropic used the classic dual-class + trust architecture (inspired by Meta, Google, Palantir, NYT-style structures) to separate economic rights from control rights. Class F locks 50.1% of general votes with the seven co-founders; Class T lets the LTBT appoint the board majority. This is the concrete institutional answer to the question Amodei has been writing about since at least the “Pace the Frontier” essay: how to keep safety pacing decisions inside the company even after outside capital enters. The mechanism works only as long as the seven founders remain aligned with each other and with the trust. It does not protect against internal disagreement, against the need to raise future capital, or against the political pressure Amodei also mentioned. Markets tested the structure on day one by giving a solid but not extreme pop and then taking some gains back; the real test will be the first time the company announces a delay or a restricted release that is visibly costly.

## Additional evidence expansion (to meet depth requirement)

### Detailed dual-class mechanics from multiple independent summaries
From Yahoo Finance / Fortune analysis: The public gets Class A shares. One share of Class F stock is issued to an LLC owned by the seven co-founders. That single share carries 50.1% of the vote and has no economic rights. Issuing new stock cannot dilute it. Class A holders together can never hold more than 49.9% of the vote. The filing language quoted across sources: Class A holders “generally will not be able to affect the outcome of any matter submitted to a vote of our stockholders or the election of our directors, regardless of how they vote.”

From Morningstar: LTBT holds a single Class T share that elects four of seven directors and can remove them without cause. Founder LLC elects the remaining three. Inside the LLC, each of the seven co-founders has one vote; a simple majority decides how Class F is cast. Four of seven can therefore determine the outcome of ordinary shareholder matters. If the majority cannot agree, some descriptions state the Class F share then votes proportionately with Class A.

From CNBC (Oct 26 reporting on the filing): The seven co-founders are sole members of Founder LLC. The structure gives them the ability to determine the outcome of all matters submitted to a vote for the foreseeable future. Amazon and Google hold large economic stakes but the voting power remains concentrated.

### Revenue and loss figures cross-checked
Guardian: $16.2bn in first six months of 2026 vs $4.6bn for all of 2025. Losses of $80bn in H1 largely from one-off charge; losses grew to $104bn in first nine months.

Multiple S-1 digests converge on: Q3 2026 revenue approximately $17.0bn (1,127% YoY in some counts); nine-month 2026 revenue $33.2bn; run-rate revenue ~$81bn as of 31 October 2026 (defined as trailing 28-day consumption fees × 13 + monthly subscription fees × 12). Compute and infrastructure expense was a majority of revenue in recent quarters. Aggregate committed spend reported in the range of $900bn, with tens of billions due within 12 months.

These figures appear in at least four independent write-ups that cite the prospectus (Kadoa Company Tracker, tldr.money, sec-api.io insight, AlphaSense primer). Slight differences exist in rounding and which amendment is cited, but no source contradicts the order of magnitude or the growth trajectory.

### Structural tension that remains unresolved
Even with voting control locked, the company has committed to roughly $900bn of future compute and infrastructure. The $81.3bn raised is large but covers only a fraction of those commitments. Future capital raises (equity or debt) will still be sensitive to the stock price. A prolonged period of underperformance or a market-wide AI sell-off could therefore constrain the practical ability to “pace” even if the legal voting power remains with the founders. This is the concrete mechanism by which public-market pressure can still reach the company despite the dual-class design.

No source located in this pass shows the founders or the LTBT having published a binding, externally enforceable rule that forces them to delay a model release if certain risk thresholds are met. The Responsible Scaling Policy exists and has been revised; the dual-class structure protects the ability to follow it, but does not automatically compel following it.
